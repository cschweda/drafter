# Part One

# The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter

## Low Tide

The tide went out farther than Wren had ever seen it. By the time the sun cleared the headland the whole bay lay bare, a grey floor of ribbed sand and stranded kelp, and the fishing boats leaned on their keels like tired animals.

Her father had warned her about mornings like this. "The sea takes a breath before it speaks," he used to say, and then he would tap the barometer twice, as if it were a friend who might be talking out of turn.

The lighthouse stood behind her, white and patient. It had been her father's to keep, and then, because there was no one else, hers.

She walked down the long iron stairs with a basket on her arm. Somewhere below, a gull complained about nothing in particular. The air smelled of salt and cold stone.

## What the Sand Kept

At the bottom of the stairs she found the bottle. It was green, thick-necked, and sealed with wax the colour of a plum. Inside, rolled tight, was a page.

Wren did not open it. Not yet. She turned it over in her hands, felt the weight of it, and thought about how many days the glass must have travelled to end up here, on this one beach, at her feet.

She tucked it into the basket beneath a cloth, the way you hide a secret from the wind.

* * *

By noon the wind had come back, and with it the sound of the bell from the village. Three slow strokes, then a pause, then three more. It was the signal for a visitor, and visitors were rare.

# A Letter in Another Hand

## The Page

She read it at the kitchen table, with the door shut and the kettle ticking as it cooled. The handwriting leaned hard to the right, as if every word were walking into a gale.

*To whoever keeps the light,* it began. *If you are reading this, the north channel has moved again. Do not trust the old charts.*

The ink had run in two places, where a wave or a tear had touched it, and she could not tell which.

Wren read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases, exactly as it had been, and slid it into the pocket of her father's coat, which still hung by the door.

## The Visitor

The man at the gate wore a coat too thin for the season. He carried no bag. He gave his name as Alder, and he said it the way people do who have used many others.

"I am told you have a light that still turns," he said.

"It turns," said Wren. "It does not always shine."

He smiled at that, a small, careful smile, and she decided, against her better judgment, to let him in.

# The Night Watch

## Climbing

The lamp room was ninety-one steps above the kitchen. Wren counted them every time, because her father had, and because counting was a way of keeping the dark at a distance.

The stairs wound upward in a spiral, and every ninth step had a window cut into the thick wall, a small square of sea that grew smaller and greyer as they climbed.

Alder followed, one hand on the rail. He was breathing hard by the sixtieth step and pretending not to.

## The Turning Light

At the top, the great lens waited under its canvas like a sleeping giant. Wren drew the cover back. The brass caught the last of the evening and threw it, gold and trembling, across the walls.

"Now," said Alder, very quietly, "tell me what the page said."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, far out where the channel had been, something large and slow moved under the water.
